Mixed Models Module

1) Mixed models: Overview

Mixed-effects models, also called mixed models, random-effects models, multilevel models, or hierarchical models, account for observations that share a group or sampling unit.

Common biological applications include:

  • Field studies: multiple samples within sites, permanent plots surveyed over time, or multiple/repeated observations from individual animals or plants.
  • Laboratory studies: repeated measurements from individual mice or lizards, cells or tissues sampled within animals, or observations sharing cultures, donors, plates, cages, or experimental runs.

Observations within a group/location/individual may be more similar than those from different groups/locations/individuals. Treating them as independent can underrepresent uncertainty and lead to pseudoreplication.

Fixed effects describe systematic comparisons or relationships, such as treatment differences or time trends. Random effects describe variation among groups/locations/individuals in baseline responses or relationships with predictors.

Model Typical application
Linear mixed model (LMM), also called a linear mixed-effects model (LME or LMEM) Continuous responses with approximately Gaussian residuals after accounting for predictors and grouping.
Generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) Counts, binary outcomes, or other responses modeled with a GLM distribution and random effects.

  • Repeated-measures and longitudinal (sampled repeatedly over time) analyses describe applications of mixed models.

  • Note: Choosing the response distribution (i.e., whether you should use a generalized linear model; review the GLM module here) and deciding whether to include random effect(s) in your model are separate decisions.

This module will help you recognize when mixed models may be appropriate, describe your design, and find a relevant analysis example.

3) Describe your study design and use AI to develop a model

AI can help you decide whether a mixed-effects model is appropriate, translate your study design into an R model formula, and find relevant tutorials.

Step 1: Describe your study and how you collected the data

Copy and adapt the prompt below. Describe what you actually did in the field or lab without worrying about statistical terminology.

Prompt to copy:

Help me choose a statistical model for my biology study, including whether I need random effects and how to specify them.

  • My biological question: [What am I trying to find out or compare?]
  • What I measured: [Describe the outcome and units. For proportions, give the number of successes and total trials; for counts, describe sampling time, area, or other effort.]
  • What one spreadsheet row represents: [For example, one quadrat survey or one mouse measured on one date. You can also paste the output of glimpse(main_data_table) here.]
  • Treatments or other factors: [What did I manipulate or compare? Explain how treatments were assigned and whether they were applied to individual animals, entire tanks, plots, or other units.]
  • How samples are organized: [Describe the numbers of sites, plots, animals, cages, cultures, or batches and samples per group. Explain which units belong together (did you use “blocks” of each treatment?) and how their IDs appear in the spreadsheet.]
  • Repeated measurements: [Did I measure the same units again at certain time intervals, take several measurements within each unit, or collect new samples each time?]
  • Timing and other details: [Describe sampling dates, missing observations, unequal sample sizes, or differences in effort.]

Before giving me R code, summarize my design back to me in plain language. Identify which units provide separate treatment comparisons and which observations are probably not independent. Ask me questions if important details are missing.

Examples of design descriptions:

  • Ecology: We counted invertebrates in four permanent plots at each of six sites, three sites for each habitat type. We surveyed the same plots monthly for six months to compare habitats.
  • Laboratory: We assigned eight cages to two diets, with four cages per diet and three mice per cage. We weighed the same mice weekly for six weeks to compare growth between diets.

Step 2: Ask for a starting model and plain-language explanations

Once the AI accurately describes your study, follow up with:

Prompt to copy:

Suggest a starting model formula in R and explain each part in plain language:

  • Model terms: Which terms describe treatment differences or other relationships (fixed effects), and which account for the fact that measurements from the same site, animal, or other group may not be independent (random effects)?
  • Type of response: What model family or distribution fits what I measured? Explain this separately from how observations are grouped.

Quick tip for students: Why “plain language” works better here:

  • When students try to use statistical terms they are still learning (like calling a fixed treatment a “random block” or saying data are “nested” when they are “crossed”), the AI will often take those terms literally and recommend the wrong model formula. Describing the physical reality of the experiment (“I had 4 tanks per temperature, and I measured 10 fish in each tank”) forces the AI to translate from biology to statistical terminology.

Step 3: Connect to a tutorial

Choose a resource from Section 5, then prompt in AI:

Review this tutorial: [paste URL]. Identify the sections or examples most relevant to my design, explain similarities and differences, and identify anything it does not address.

Step 4: Review the tutorial and adapt code to your analysis

Your analysis should still involve all the typical steps: exploring data distributions and individual or group patterns with ggplot2, fitting the model, checking diagnostics, and interpreting estimates or predictions with confidence intervals. If you are unsure, you can ask AI to help you interpret a model or diagnostic output (paste the R code and the output into the AI). Check the sources directly and discuss important choices with your adviser.

4) R packages for fitting mixed models

These are the R packages typically used for fitting linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) and generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs). You can typically start with whichever package is used in the tutorial you are following.

Package and main functions Why use it?
lme4: lmer(), glmer() Common starting point for LMMs and GLMMs; nested and crossed random effects. Paper/citation for this package.
glmmTMB: glmmTMB() Flexible families, including negative binomial; options for zero-inflation, dispersion, and correlation structures.

5) Choose a tutorial relevant to your research

Here is a list of tutorials walking through linear mixed-effects model analyses. Find the one most appropriate to adapt to your needs (you can use AI to help identify it).

Tutorial Description
(Sci. Paper) Harrison et al. (2018): A brief introduction to mixed effects modelling and multi-model inference in ecology Paper that walks through examples and explanations for how mixed models can be used in ecological research.
Coding Club: Introduction to linear mixed models A workshop aimed at people new to mixed modeling. As such, it doesn’t cover all the nuances of mixed models, but hopefully serves as a starting point.
PopEcol: Ecology-focused tutorial on linear mixed-effects models A step-by-step R tutorial that uses an intuitive ecological field example (multiple bird nests sampled across individual trees) to show how ignoring grouped data leads to pseudoreplication and false positives, and how to specify random intercepts, nested designs, and random slopes.
Bench Biostats: Models for non-independence An online textbook (mixed-model chapter) focused on using laboratory experiments as examples, including subsampling, blocked designs, and batches.
QCBS R Workshop 7: Linear and Generalized Linear Mixed Models An online textbook guide focused on ecology (using an example of fish trophic position across species and lakes) that covers how to avoid pseudoreplication, build LMMs and GLMMs in lme4, check model assumptions, and plot predictions with ggplot2.
Bridges Lab: Using mixed linear models for repeated measures Using longitudinal data, this R tutorial demonstrates how to account for repeated measurements on the same individuals over time by progressing from a standard linear model to mixed-effects models.
Applied Biostats Online Textbook, Random Effects and Mixed Model Chapters Relevant chapters using tidyverse workflows that introduce mixed models, fixed versus random effects, and tools like broom.mixed and ggeffects for tidying and plotting model outputs.
DHARMa Package Vignette: Residual Diagnostics for Hierarchical Models Companion guide for checking assumptions (overdispersion, zero-inflation, and residual patterns) in mixed models fitted with lme4 or glmmTMB using simulation-based residuals.
Official glmmTMB introduction Grouped count data and GLMM extensions; ecological count example with repeated observations of nests. Consult advanced options as needed.

And remember, if your response variable is count or binary data, you can connect these examples to the Generalized Linear Models module and DataCamp’s GLMM chapter.

6) Explore, fit, check, and interpret your model

Follow the same general workflow as for other models:

  1. Explore: use ggplot2 histograms, individual-value plots, and scatterplots; show groups and patterns, and summarize replication.
  2. Specify and fit: match the response distribution and fixed and random effects to your question and design.
  3. Check: examine residual patterns, assumptions, warnings, and remaining temporal/spatial dependence. Assess residual normality for LMMs and distribution-appropriate diagnostics for GLMMs, typically with the DHARMa package.
  4. Interpret and plot: emphasize effect sizes, predictions or estimated means, confidence intervals, and biological relevance.
  5. Report: describe sampling units, group and observation counts, model structure, estimation, diagnostics, and packages.

Ben Bolker and colleagues’ GLMM FAQ: You can use this reference for model specification, diagnostics, convergence warnings, singular fits, and other troubleshooting.
